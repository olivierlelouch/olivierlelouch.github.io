About Me

I am an Economist and PhD Candidate at UCL, specializing in the intersection of sovereign debt sustainability and the macroeconomics of the energy transition.

My research examines how macroeconomic policy can support fiscal resilience and economic stability during the global shift toward low-carbon economies. I am currently working on similar questions as a visiting researcher at the Banque de France.

Previously, I led and contributed to policy and analytical work at the World Bank as a Macroeconomist in the Fiscal Unit, focusing on fiscal aspects of low-carbon policies. I also worked at the International Energy Agency (IEA) as an Analyst in the Oil Industry Market Division.

I hold a Master’s degree from the University of Paris-Saclay in Energy Economics and a ‘Grande École’ Engineering Master’s degree from IMT Atlantique (formerly École des Mines de Nantes & Télécom Bretagne) in France.

Work in progress

  • Ongoing

    Induced Green Technical Change and Public Finances

    C. Seghini and O. Lelouch. Banque de France Working Paper.

    Summary

    Studies how endogenous green technical change shapes the transition to a low-carbon economy, and what that transition does to public finances and sovereign risk. It compares policies that encourage green research and development directly, through subsidies, tax credits and public R&D, with those that do so indirectly, through the carbon price, deployment subsidies and the use of carbon revenue. The starting point is the estimated closed-economy New Keynesian model of Seghini and Dees (2026), which includes green and fossil energy and sovereign default risk; the experiment replaces its temporary carbon tax shock with a permanent increase in the carbon tax from one steady state to another. The extension is designed to be portable, adding few enough equations to carry over to GMMET, the institutional benchmark model.

  • Ongoing

    Public Finances, Sovereign Credit Risk, Sovereign Debt Sustainability and the Financing of the Low-Carbon Transition

    O. Lelouch, University College London. Work in progress.

    Summary

    The MINDSET model is structurally designed for capturing transmission channels and transition risks associated with low-carbon transition policies. It combines the strengths of input-output analysis with those of a demand-led macro-econometric model: the multi-regional input-output network links sectors and regions, while the macro-econometric equations determine the behavioural dynamics of final demand. MINDSET captures most economic costs associated with mitigation policies, the overall financial impact through credit availability, and macroeconomic impacts through losses in aggregate demand. It is enhanced with a sovereign debt default model, based on Bassa and Cont (2025) and Seghini and Dees (2025), in which sovereign default probability spreads through the economy via a feedback loop in the private sector lending rate. Carbon pricing policies and green public investment are assessed.

  • Ongoing

    Sustainability-Linked Instruments for Sovereigns and Public Sector Issuers

    G. L. Frisari, O. Lelouch, L. Rayman and D. Wang. World Bank Staff Position Note.

    Summary

    Sets out the World Bank’s approach to assessing and structuring sustainability-linked finance, in which financial terms depend on the issuer meeting environmental, social or governance targets rather than on how the proceeds are spent. The note weighs the flexibility these instruments offer fiscally constrained sovereigns against their structuring costs and their risk of fragmenting the yield curve, and identifies the preconditions for successful issuance. It then covers the selection of financially material indicators and ambitious targets, credit enhancement and payoff design, drawing on examples from Chile, Uruguay and Barbados.

  • Ongoing

    Sovereign Debt Buybacks: A Strategic Tool for Resilience

    C. Laskaridis and O. Lelouch. CETEx, London School of Economics.

    Summary

    Sovereign debt buybacks have returned to the agenda as a response to debt distress in low- and middle-income countries, and debt-for-climate and debt-for-nature swaps, such as recent deals in Ecuador and Barbados, are at their core buyback transactions financed with guarantees from multilateral development banks. As scrutiny of these swaps grows, the paper draws on decades of experience with sovereign debt buybacks to extract lessons on their potential effectiveness and pitfalls.
